[![RoboTrack wordmark logo on a dark teal-to-magenta gradient background](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407244-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-1.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407244-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-1.png)

Deploying robots in real-world settings requires reliable perception throughout an interaction. To pick something up, a robot needs to follow the positions of its gripper and the object as they move. In a laboratory, a robot handling a pipette must keep track of its tip while aligning it with the opening of a tube. These tasks become harder when nearby objects clutter the view, the gripper obscures the object being handled, or the viewing angle changes.

One way to help robots follow these interactions is to track specific locations on the objects they handle. This is the idea behind *point tracking*. Given a location in one video frame, a tracker estimates where that same point appears in subsequent frames, when it is occluded, and where it reappears. These trajectories can help robots learn from demonstrations, plan and guide movements, and model how objects respond to actions.

Robotics systems often rely on off-the-shelf trackers to automatically annotate motion in videos and provide feedback during execution. And current trackers score well on established robotics benchmarks, suggesting they could be ready for wider use in real-world systems. We wanted to see whether those results hold up across the varied scenes, viewpoints, and object interactions robots encounter in practice.

We introduce [**RoboTrack**](https://robotrack.allen.ai/), a benchmark and training data suite for point tracking in robotic manipulation. [RoboTrack-Bench](https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/RoboTrack-Bench) contains 307 human-annotated videos from DROID, Ego-Exo4D, [MolmoAct 2](https://allenai.org/blog/molmoact2), and EgoVerse, covering different robots and human hands, cluttered scenes, and first- and third-person viewpoints. To study how training data affects tracking in these settings, we also extend [MolmoSpaces](https://allenai.org/blog/molmospaces), Ai2’s simulation ecosystem for robot learning, to generate dense point tracks from simulated robot manipulation trajectories. This pipeline produces [RoboTrack-15M](https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/RoboTrack-15M), a new synthetic training dataset targeted toward robotic manipulation.

[![Overview diagram of RoboTrack showing RoboTrack-15M simulated data generation from MolmoSpaces and RoboTrack-Bench human-annotated real-world manipulation footage](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407698-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-2.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407698-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-2.png)

*RoboTrack is a benchmark and training data suite for point tracking in robotic manipulation. Built on MolmoSpaces, RoboTrack-15M (top-left) generates point tracks for training. Training with this simulated data improves tracking on real manipulation footage. RoboTrack-Bench (top-right) provides human-annotated point tracks in real manipulation videos.*

Our evaluations show that leading trackers score much lower on RoboTrack-Bench than on existing robotics benchmarks. Adding RoboTrack-15M to an existing tracker’s training improves performance on real manipulation video, with gains extending to other tracking benchmarks. We bring these pieces together on a real robot, using the resulting tracker to help guide pipette handling in wet-lab tasks.

Today, we’re releasing both RoboTrack components and two new point tracking models: [RoboTracker and RoboTracker-Warp](https://huggingface.co/collections/allenai/robotrack). RoboTracker is a fine-tuned AllTracker model using our techniques and data, while RoboTracker-Warp is trained from CoWTracker. Both models improve average AJ across the three robotics benchmarks compared with their respective released baselines, and RoboTracker-Warp achieves state-of-the-art performance on four out of the five benchmarks we evaluated. We’re also releasing our [paper](https://allenai.org/papers/robotrack) describing the work and evaluation results.

## RoboTrack-Bench: A benchmark for point-tracking in real-world manipulation

[![Examples of human-verified point tracks in RoboTrack-Bench across DROID, Ego-Exo4D, MolmoAct 2, and EgoVerse](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407981-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-3-1.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795407981-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-3-1.png)

*RoboTrack-Bench at a glance. Examples from each source dataset (307 clips and 2,522 human-verified tracks in total). MolmoAct 2 and EgoVerse show one embodiment per panel. DROID and EgoExo4D show synchronized ego (top) and exo (bottom) views.*

RoboTrack-Bench expands evaluation beyond controlled tabletop setups. It contains 307 videos from DROID, Ego-Exo4D, MolmoAct 2, and EgoVerse, annotated with a total of 2,522 point tracks. The videos show robots and human hands manipulating objects across cluttered scenes and first- and third-person viewpoints. Small objects can be difficult to follow against a busy background, and grippers often hide the objects they hold.

Every track in RoboTrack-Bench is annotated and verified by humans. We ask annotators to prioritize points that move or become occluded rather than the many static background points in robot videos. Following the TAP-Vid annotation approach, annotators use motion estimates to help trace each point through a video. They label each track as belonging to a robot or human body, a manipulated object, or a static object to support future analysis of performance on each group. A second group of annotators checks every track frame by frame before it’s included in our curated data.

To support research in multi-view pointing, correspondence, and tracking, RoboTrack-Bench contains a subset of 33 video pairs that were recorded simultaneously from two cameras. We annotate the same physical points in both views, synchronized across frames, connecting different views of the same interaction.

## Point trackers struggle on real-world manipulation

We evaluated four leading trackers – CoTracker3, BootsTAPNext, AllTracker, and CoWTracker – on RoboTrack-Bench and four other benchmarks: [RoboTAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.15975) (real-world tabletop manipulation), RGB-Stacking (simulated manipulation), and DAVIS and Kinetics (general video). We report Average Jaccard (AJ), which measures tracking accuracy and visibility prediction; higher scores are better.

[![Evaluation table comparing CoTracker3, BootsTAPNext, AllTracker, and CoWTracker in Average Jaccard across RoboTrack-Bench, RoboTAP, RGB-Stacking, DAVIS, and Kinetics](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408316-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-4.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408316-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-4.png)

**RoboTrack-Bench is harder than existing robot benchmarks**. Every final tracker we evaluated scores lower on RoboTrack-Bench than on RoboTAP, the only other real-video robotics benchmark. Their score drops by 3.0 to 6.6 AJ. Even the strongest off-the-shelf tracker, CoWTracker, drops from 70.5 AJ on RoboTAP to 63.9 on RoboTrack-Bench. The drop holds across models despite their different architectures and training data, indicating RoboTrack-Bench identifies weaknesses across multiple trackers.

**RGB-Stacking rewards synthetic training more than real-world ability.** BootsTAPNext is TAPNext trained further on 15 million real videos. That extra real-world training raises its RoboTrack-Bench score by 5.1 AJ (55.1 to 60.2), but lowers its RGB-Stacking score by 9.5 AJ (75.0 to 65.5). RGB-Stacking is built from simulated block stacking, and it ranks the Kubric-only model higher, while real manipulation video ranks them the other way around.

**A lead on the older benchmarks does not carry over.** CoWTracker leads CoTracker3 by 4.7 AJ on RoboTAP, but by only 2.1 AJ on RoboTrack-Bench. AllTracker’s 2.7-point lead over CoTracker3 on RoboTAP shrinks to 0.8. BootsTAPNext, the strongest model on DAVIS, falls 3.7 AJ behind CoWTracker on RoboTrack-Bench and even trails the Kubric-only AllTracker (60.2 vs 61.4 AJ), despite its training on 15 million real videos.

These results suggest that existing benchmarks give an incomplete picture of tracking performance in real-world manipulation. Evaluating on more varied scenes reveals difficulties shared by all four trackers and changes how their strengths compare.

*Note: Each tracker runs with its authors’ settings and is all scored in the same way. Our AllTracker scores differ slightly from the published ones because we fixed a scoring bug in its released evaluation code. Additionally, we evaluate RoboTAP and Kinetics on all annotated points rather than following AllTracker’s protocol. See our paper for full evaluation protocols.*

## RoboTrack-15M: Simulation data from MolmoSpaces

Why do these trackers struggle with real-world manipulation? Many trackers train on synthetic videos generated with [Kubric](https://github.com/google-research/kubric), which shows objects tossed into a scene. These provide examples of motion and occlusion, but don’t focus on robots grasping and moving objects. We wanted to test whether adding trajectories from these interactions could improve tracking on real manipulation videos.

This is where MolmoSpaces comes in—our robotic simulation environment at Ai2. We extend its existing manipulation simulations to generate dense point tracks on the robot and the objects it handles. In each episode, a simulated robot arm picks up an everyday object, such as a cup, a plate, or a block. Two cameras record the interaction: one on the wrist and one at the robot’s base, rendered in MuJoCo. Using the simulator’s geometry, object poses, and camera information, we calculate each point’s position and visibility in every frame without human annotation and filter out static points on the background and the robot’s fixed base.

The pipeline produces RoboTrack-15M for training. RoboTrack-15M has 3,000 videos from 300 simulated homes with 5,000 tracks per video, for 15 million tracks in total.

[![Diagram of the RoboTrack-15M synthetic data generation pipeline built on MolmoSpaces and MuJoCo](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408512-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-5.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408512-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-5.png)

*Data generation pipeline of RoboTrack-15M. Objects, indoor scenes, and robot grasps are obtained from MolmoSpaces. We use the simulator’s geometry and camera information to calculate point positions and visibility.*

## RoboTracker: Point tracker trained on simulated manipulation

We introduce two models trained on RoboTrack-15M, RoboTracker and RoboTracker-Warp. Each fine-tunes an existing tracker on RoboTrack-15M together with its original training data. RoboTracker builds on AllTracker, a lightweight model with 17 million parameters, and RoboTracker-Warp builds on CoWTracker, a much larger model with about 1 billion parameters.

We’re releasing both model checkpoints so other researchers can inspect and build on this work. RoboTracker-Warp’s weights are for noncommercial use.

**Adding RoboTrack-15M improves scores on robotics benchmarks.** Compared with the released AllTracker and CoWTracker models, respectively, RoboTracker improves RoboTrack-Bench by 0.8 AJ (62.6 to 63.4) and RoboTracker-Warp by 2.1 AJ (63.9 to 66.0), with gains of up to 1.2 AJ averaged across the three robotics benchmarks. On general video benchmarks, both models perform on par with their base models. Overall, RoboTracker-Warp has the highest AJ of the models we evaluated on four of the five benchmarks. The exception is DAVIS, where BootsTAPNext and CoWTracker score slightly higher.

[![Chart of RoboTrack-Bench results broken down by video source dataset](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408710-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-7.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408710-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-7.png)

Breaking the gains down by source shows where they come from. RoboTracker-Warp improves on every source by roughly 1 to 3 AJ, including the human footage from EgoVerse and Ego-Exo4D, even though RoboTrack-15M contains only robot arms. RoboTracker’s gains are smaller and come mostly from robot footage—clearest on DROID Franka and EgoVerse EVA.

## What makes good simulation data for point tracking?

RoboTrack-15M is the result of multiple iterations and experiments with our synthetic data. In fact, our first attempt didn’t bring any gain on RoboTrack-Bench. To find out why, we looked at a simple question: how far do the tracked points move compared with real manipulation videos? We plot this as a histogram for each version of our data and measure its distance from RoboTrack-Bench (figure below).

[![Histograms comparing point motion distributions and Jensen-Shannon divergence across training data iterations and RoboTrack-Bench for third-person and first-person views](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408926-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-8.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408926-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-8.png)

*How far points move in each version of our training data compared with RoboTrack-Bench, for third-person (top) and first-person (bottom) views. Distance is the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between the two histograms, with 0 indicating identical histograms. Kubric has one camera type, so the same clips appear in both rows. AJ is measured on the full RoboTrack-Bench.*

The histograms made it clear what was going wrong. Our first version picked points anywhere in the frame, including walls, floors, and furniture, so most points seen from the fixed third-person camera never moved. To address this, we next sampled points only on the robot and the objects it handles, which brought the wrist view close to real video and raised the score. Interestingly, we found that Kubric had the same mismatch in the wrist view, which likely explains why it alone isn’t enough for the varied viewpoints of robotic manipulation. Finally, we moved points from the robot’s fixed base and mounting platform onto the moving arm, which brings our third-person view in line with real video as well and raises the ablation score to 63.0 AJ, 1.5 points above the Kubric-only control.

## Application: Robotic manipulation using point tracking

[![Diagram of the single-demonstration wet-lab pipetting pipeline guided by RoboTracker point tracks](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795409178-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-9-4.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795409178-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-9-4.png)

*Our wet-lab pipeline for guiding the robot with point tracks from a single demonstration.*

What does better point tracking mean for robotics? Many robotic systems have found point tracks useful as a compact representation of how objects move that can guide a policy directly. To see this in practice, we built a similar system for a wet-lab task: pipetting liquid from one vial to another.

Here, a robot arm picks up an electronic pipette, draws liquid from the first vial, dispenses it into the second, and returns the pipette to its holder. The task is challenging because the pipette tip has to line up with the vial opening within millimeters, and the robot needs to adjust to different layouts of vials in each trial.

Following RoboTap, the robot learns the task from a single demonstration, which we record by teleoperation and split into steps. At test time, the robot compares what its camera sees with the demonstration and uses RoboTracker to estimate the motion required to align with the vial. Because the following motion runs without further feedback, the task only succeeds if that estimate is accurate.

We compare three ways of estimating where the vial has moved:

- Optical flow (RAFT) matches pixels directly between the demonstration image and the live camera image.
- Object masks (SAM3) find the vial in each new image and line up the center of its mask with the demonstration.
- Point tracking (RoboTracker) follows points on the vial throughout the video, matching points in the demonstration image to the live image.

We ran each method on 20 layouts with the vials in different positions: 10 with a clear view and 10 with partial occlusion. Point tracking completed the full transfer in 17 of 20 trials, compared with 8 for object masks and 5 for optical flow. It succeeded in all 10 clear-view trials and 7 of 10 with occlusion, where the mask-based method succeeded only once.

The other methods fail in different ways. Optical flow breaks down when the vials sit far from where they were in the demonstration, and masks can latch onto a similar-looking object or lose the vial when it’s occluded. Point tracking, on the other hand, follows the same vial object from frame to frame, so it handles both large moves and partial occlusion. These results suggest point tracking is a promising tool for precise robotic manipulation tasks.

[![Bar chart showing wet-lab pipetting trial success rates for optical flow, object masks, and RoboTracker point tracking with and without occlusion](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408826-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-9.png)](https://www.datocms-assets.com/64837/1795408826-robotrack-blog-draft-google-docs-image-9.png)

*Success rate of pipetting trials with each method, with and without occlusion (10 layouts each). A trial counts as a success when the robot completes the full transfer.*

## Limitations and what’s next

We find the trackers still struggle with long occlusions, finding points when they reappear, and following small objects against busy backgrounds. Training on RoboTrack-15M improves performance, but these failures remain a challenge for real-world use.

Our simulations also focus on picking up everyday objects. They don't yet capture the precise movements involved in tasks such as pipette handling. A next step is to simulate these tasks more closely and test whether training on their trajectories improves tracking during deployment.

RoboTrack gives us a way to measure these failures and generate training data to address them. We hope the benchmarks, simulation pipeline, and released model help others build trackers that robots can rely on.
