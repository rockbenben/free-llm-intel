# AI readiness assessment: Is your enterprise prepared?

![AI readiness assessment: Is your enterprise ready?](https://cdn.sanity.io/images/rjtqmwfu/web3-prod/2fe8f3315b7d7b2dde0fec7473be95dd5249460f-3840x2160.png?auto=format&fit=max&q=80&w=1130)

Is your organization truly ready to unleash the potential of AI? That’s a big question — and an urgent one for enterprise leaders. It may seem like it’s all about adopting the right AI technology. In reality, AI readiness encompasses a wide range of dimensions that are each critical for success.

Yet many enterprises are rushing ahead with AI initiatives without a clear understanding of their organization’s current capabilities, risks, and skills gaps. Others are starting from zero with a desire to adopt AI, but without a clear idea of how to take the first step.

A structured AI readiness assessment can give leaders a roadmap to turn ambitious AI visions into measurable, sustainable business impact. This assessment serves as a pre-adoption diagnostic that can help organizations prepare mindfully before embarking on their own unique path towards [enterprise AI maturity](https://cohere.com/blog/enterprise-ai-maturity-model) with confidence.

## What is AI readiness?

Enterprise AI readiness refers to an organization’s holistic state of preparedness that will enable it to deploy, adopt, and manage AI technologies in a way that delivers organizational value and business/mission outcomes while mitigating risk. But it’s not just about having the latest AI tools in place. AI readiness encompasses a wide range of factors across strategy, infrastructure, data, governance, and organizational change.

### AI readiness for regulated industries

For enterprises in regulated industries, such as finance, healthcare, and the public sector, additional AI readiness considerations come into play. These organizations need even greater control over AI implementations in order to meet their sovereignty, data residency, and compliance requirements, adding a deeper dimension to their strategy and planning.

Our recent [IDC InfoBrief on sovereign AI adoption](https://cohere.com/blog/state-of-sovereign-ai-adoption-2026), however, reveals that an understanding of sovereignty’s definition, requirements, and readiness lags in many regulated organizations. It shows that today only 13% of leaders are “very widely” aware of sovereign AI, meaning that technology decision-makers may be making AI investments without sufficient context.

## The AI readiness framework: Seven dimensions to assess

An organization can be strong in some areas of AI readiness and weak in others, with the weakest dimension usually being the bottleneck or risk to moving forward. The effective AI readiness assessment can be broken down into seven distinct areas:

- Strategy and leadership alignment
- AI technical expertise resources
- Technology and infrastructure
- Governance, risk, and compliance
- Data readiness for AI
- Organizational change
- Use case discovery, prioritization, and value

### Strategy and leadership alignment

Many organizations relegate the decision-making, planning, and rollout of AI to their technology leaders. However, business sponsorship is also critical to success. Senior leadership alignment around strategy, vision, funding, and ownership is often a blocker to AI readiness.

Organizations must designate an executive leader to ensure that the right strategy is in place to deliver impact and ROI, as well as ensure that all dimensions of the AI readiness framework have been addressed before moving forward. In addition, an effective AI strategy must include an understanding of the [ROI of AI](https://cohere.com/blog/how-do-we-measure-the-roi-of-generative-ai-2), as well as include KPIs to measure performance and results.

### AI technical expertise resources

Enterprises are often faced with legacy technical skillsets that don't translate well to AI, or they don’t have enough AI expertise in house to execute AI use cases. Others place a heavier burden on non-technical employees by upskilling them to implement some self-service use cases, with only occasional technical support needed. Note that this may bring some change management challenges that can hinder success,

**The growing demand for FDEs**

[Forward deployed engineers (FDEs)](https://cohere.com/blog/forward-deployed-engineers-capability-building) are a great solution for many enterprises who are not able to bring the right talent in-house. These temporary, external resources sit alongside both business and technical employees to help them design and implement AI initiatives.

In the AI readiness phase, FDEs can also advise leaders across various dimensions discussed in this post. FDEs can be contracted through AI technology providers (like Cohere), bringing the added benefit of expertise in their company’s models, APIs, and platforms.

Eventually, organizations will want to move beyond FDEs and build up their own in-house AI Ops team for ongoing support. Doing so involves reimaging their team structures to scope new role requirements, identifying core skillsets that will drive success. They’ll need to plan for a slow transition away from external support until the new in-house team becomes self-sufficient.

### Technology and infrastructure

AI workloads require specialized compute, storage, and orchestration to operate effectively. A robust [AI infrastructure](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-infrastructure) synchronizes hardware, software, and data workflows, ensuring that AI systems perform reliably against business objectives and support the scale, speed, and complexity of modern AI. Without this foundation, organizations struggle to operationalize business critical AI applications. It’s important to note that not every task needs a frontier model. The key to driving ROI is [right-sizing AI to workloads](https://cohere.com/blog/how-small-models-can-make-a-big-impact-for-enterprises) and choosing the models and infrastructure that best serve them.

**Readiness for sovereign AI**

For enterprises in regulated industries, data privacy, security and compliance are non-negotiable. These organizations benefit from [private deployment](https://cohere.com/private-deployments) options, running their AI infrastructure either on-premises or in a virtual private cloud (VPC), for maximum security and control.

### Governance, risk, and compliance

AI governance is essential for helping enterprises adopt AI safely, consistently, and at scale. However, it is not simply about ticking a checkbox; responsible AI governance encompasses the entire lifecycle of AI development and deployment. Good governance involves a repeatable process by which internal teams handling AI model use cases can validate whether or not certain criteria (for transparency, security, and risk management) were met at different stages of development. This process ensures that an organization can deploy AI operations at scale as they continue to identify, mitigate, and manage AI related risks specific to their use case applications.

Previously, engineering and IT teams were the ones most involved in governing enterprise solutions through the software development lifecycle (SDLC). And while existing SDLC processes can still be the foundation for governing in the AI landscape, the step-level change of AI, both in terms of how it executes (probabilistic vs. deterministic like code) and the individuals who build with it, requires re-evaluation of those processes to account for new risk surfaces. It introduces a new emphasis on what we refer to as AIOps: the AI-driven development lifecycle. In this lifecycle of AI product development, processes are an increasingly important factor in building and maintaining reliance and trust on the organization’s AI solutions. As models change and harnesses improve, AIOps teams must continue to validate solutions to prevent model drift, data drift, and hallucinations.

Technical teams cannot work in silos within an organization. For GRC processes to be successful, leadership must set organization-wide policies for the [responsible use and management of AI technologies](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-governance-challenges). An organization-level policy on responsible AI use also ensures compliance with [appropriate regulations](https://trustcenter.cohere.com/) and standards, establishes clear and transparent ownership of decisions about each AI use case, and allows the organization to seamlessly expand its use of AI to deliver on new and innovative use cases into the future.

### Data readiness for AI

Sensitive data risk is not just about employees copying information into public AI tools. It can also arise when AI systems are connected to internal data sources without appropriate controls for access, retention, retrieval, logging, or downstream use.

For a company’s [data to be AI-ready](https://cohere.com/blog/from-data-chaos-to-clarity-unlock-enterprise-ai-value), it must be of high quality, well-governed through internal data policies and processes, and available for use by AI. Transparent data lineage, where data is tracked from origin to destination, is also critical in understanding how data will flow into a new AI system.

As AI becomes increasingly universal in enterprise architecture, funneling proprietary data through an organization’s AI systems can drive innovation and create significant competitive differentiation in the marketplace. Private deployment of AI can help an enterprise to ensure data sovereignty by keeping everything running behind company firewalls.

**Data-specific AI use cases**

When thinking about AI use cases, many leaders focus on automating specific employee workflows. However, AI can also be used to transform data operations at a macro scale, with system-level use cases that create exponential value across the enterprise.

### Organizational change

Employees are key to AI success at any organization, yet many leaders still treat this dimension as an afterthought. Every AI readiness assessment must include an [organization-wide change management plan](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-change-management) that can build employee excitement and trust in AI initiatives.

Going beyond putting the right talent in place, leaders need to consider which teams and individuals will be impacted and provide appropriate upskilling programs for both business and technical staff that can build confidence and reduce resistance to change. Leaders and managers must clearly define and reimagine roles and team composition, and nurture an AI-positive, employee-centric culture around [workforce transformation](https://cohere.com/blog/enterprise-ai-maturity-model-pt2).

### Use case discovery, prioritization, and value

Many leaders have a mandate to implement AI but lack vision for specifically *how* AI will create value for the organization. As a result, they tend to gravitate towards the most immediate and visible process automation, the low-hanging fruit of efficiency gains and cost reduction, which provides limited value to the business and caps ROI. Often, this is when AI pilots fizzle and the path towards ROI becomes stressful.

Moving beyond this tactical level, leaders need to also consider how AI can address bigger questions that are relevant to the organization as a whole. For example, AI can help leaders reimagine their business in order to drive new revenue opportunities, differentiation, and strategic advantage/moats that expand margins, grow the top line, and deliver greater impact in the marketplace.

Use case discovery and prioritization is a useful exercise to perform cross-functionally, as well as within individual teams or business units. The process also needs to tie each use case to a measurable business outcome and any associated risks. A sequenced rollout plan may start with higher-value, lower-risk use cases that support other readiness efforts, such as change management.

If advisory support is needed, an AI vendor and FDEs can be a valuable resource for leaders and teams. At Cohere, for example, we work closely with customers on use case ideation, prioritization, feasibility assessment, scoping, prototyping, piloting, and adoption.

## Why AI readiness matters now for enterprise leaders

As enterprise leaders see their competitors integrating AI into products and operations, the urgency to do the same grows exponentially. According to [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-23-gartner-survey-reveals-80-percent-of-ceos-say-artificial-intelligence-will-force-operational-capability-overhauls), 80% of CEOs surveyed “expect AI to force a high to medium degree of change to their operational capabilities.” But the cost of scaling on a weak foundation is high, both in wasted spend and missed opportunity.

The proof of readiness is in the eventual impact and ROI of AI: can an organization move past a few pilot projects into full scale transformation in a sustainable way that delivers maximum business value and measurable results? And, can it build AI strategies and operations that are repeatable across business units and geographies?

### From AI pilots to business impact: The AI maturity model

Our [analysis of the AI maturity model](https://cohere.com/blog/enterprise-ai-maturity-model) shows that most organizations get stuck while attempting to scale their AI investment based on fragmented, disconnected pilots. They have yet to implement a strong, centralized infrastructure, consistent [AI governance](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-governance), and strategic alignment to effectively integrate AI into their core tech stack and organization as a whole.

If organizations do manage to deploy and scale some AI projects, many are hampered by a limited vision for AI that does not take them to the [final phase of the AI maturity journey](https://cohere.com/blog/enterprise-ai-maturity-model-pt2). They use AI to do the same things slightly faster instead of realizing AI’s full potential to transform operations and accelerate top-line revenue.

This is exactly why starting with an AI readiness assessment is a wise move. It can help leaders put all of the pieces in place *before* unleashing the full efforts of their organization to deliver the groundbreaking impact they envision from AI.

## FAQs about AI readiness

### How do you assess AI readiness?

Start with a cross‑functional workshop of key stakeholders from business and technical teams. Together, map your organization’s current capabilities against the seven AI readiness dimensions discussed in this post. Using the checklist above, assign a simple self‑score (0‑5) for each area, then prioritize gaps with the highest impact. Finally, create an action plan to address those gaps. Explore the journey beyond AI readiness in our AI maturity model.

### What is data readiness for AI?

Data readiness for AI means having clean, accessible, well‑governed data with clear lineage and permission controls. Weak data foundations are the most common reason that AI projects stall. Our post on [AI in business intelligence](https://cohere.com/blog/ai-for-business-intelligence) explains how to build a data foundation that supports AI at scale.

### What is an AI readiness framework?

An AI readiness framework is a structured set of dimensions, such as strategy, technology, governance, talent, and data, that organizations use to diagnose gaps before launching AI initiatives. It provides a common language and roadmap for moving from assessment to production to full organizational transformation. Learn more about how [AI readiness leads to the AI‑native enterprise](https://cohere.com/blog/enterprise-ai-maturity-model-pt2).
