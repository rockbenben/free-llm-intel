# How to Yield at Wafer Scale

[Isaac Tai](https://www.cerebras.ai/author/isaac-tai)

Defects and variations are inevitable in semiconductor manufacturing. Conventional processors accommodate them by disabling defective cores and sorting chips into different performance grades.

Wafer-scale computing faces the same challenges across a much larger surface area, creating more opportunities for defects and outliers. This has long raised doubts about whether wafer-scale processors could be manufactured economically.

Cerebras designed the WSE around these realities. We've [previously](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem) discussed how the WSE's 900,000 small, independent cores can provide excellent defect tolerance after fabrication through redundancy. In this article, we’ll dive into the rest of the manufacturing process, including how we:

- Design functional circuits to meet performance and power requirements despite parametric variation.
- Tune voltage independently across 84 die regions.
- Use distributed sensors and testing to characterize and monitor the wafer.
- Recover failures through software-based reconfiguration, both during manufacturing and after deployment.

Together, these capabilities enable yields that meet or even exceed those of GPUs 1/58th of our size *and* support years of continuous operation after deployment.

### Before fabrication: Designing for wide variation

Small variations in transistor dimensions, wire widths and layer alignment arise throughout chip manufacturing, affecting how circuits respond to voltage and temperature.

These differences produce **parametric variation**, so called because they change measurable electrical characteristics such as switching speed, resistance and leakage current. These differences determine whether a functional circuit can deliver the required performance within its power limits.

**Parametric yield** measures the fraction of functional processors that also meet the requirements. Designing circuits that have good parametric yield starts long before tape-out.

### Building margin into the physical design

We develop the WSE on TSMC’s established N5 process, drawing on its mature production history.

During chip design, engineers lay out transistors and their connections using the foundry’s process design kit, or PDK. It includes device models and mandatory physical-design rules governing features such as wire widths, spacing and connections between metal layers. The mandatory rules are sufficient to develop a working chip, but TSMC also provides recommended rules called Design For Manufacturability, or DFM. When followed, these add more protection against manufacturing variations.

Consider a *via*, a connection between two metal layers. While mandatory design rules specify a minimum enclosure size around a via, DFM recommends a wider one, which provides additional margin to accommodate variation in etching and metal filling. This reduces the risk of voids that can weaken the connection.

We incorporate recommendations where the protection justifies the layout cost, including configuring our routing tools to produce the intended geometry.

### **Verifying timing across a wide range of variation**

Before fabrication, engineers set a target clock frequency and use foundry-provided models to calculate how signals travel through the design across voltage and temperature variation. They revise paths that miss their requirements until the design passes. This process is **timing closure**.

Consider two registers, A and B. At each tick, A launches a new value while B captures the previous one.

- **Setup** checks that the previous value arrives and settles before capture.
- **Hold** checks that the next value arrives late enough for capture to finish reliably.

Engineers choose how much variation a design must tolerate. The industry measures this range in **sigma**, or standard deviations. Higher sigma targets require circuits to meet their timing requirements farther from the average modeled behavior. [Synopsys describes three-sigma timing signoff as typical](https://www.synopsys.com/blogs/chip-design/improving-design-robustness-primeshield-discussion-li-ding.html).

We design our circuits and layout to meet wide timing targets. The WSE targets up to **four-sigma setup and **up to** seven-sigma hold**. Through careful cell selection, routing and clock design, our team achieves these targets while giving up less than 1% of potential core capacity.

For conventional processors, [binning](https://www.tomshardware.com/reviews/glossary-binning-definition,5892.html) silicon into different performance grades can be a more economical way to accommodate variation than designing for higher sigma targets. At wafer scale, broader parametric tolerance is central to producing reliable chips economically. Our engineers invest in that tolerance during the design phase, closing timing across a wider range of conditions before the first wafer is fabricated.

### **After fabrication: Characterizing and tuning the wafer**

### Picking the best cores with edge exclusion

When a wafer returns from fabrication, yield loss is unevenly distributed across its surface. The outer edge has disproportionately higher yield loss due to processes such as polishing and film deposition that are harder to keep uniform. We can visualize this by dividing the wafer into five radial zones, each representing ~20% of its surface area.

The WSE’s square footprint leaves most of the outermost zone untouched. Approximately 97% of its active circuitry lies within the inner four zones, limiting exposure to the region responsible for much of the yield loss and variation. Where affected cores remain, including near the corners, our [repair process](https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem) can exclude them and replace them with healthier cores from our redundant reserve.

This gives us a better starting population of circuits to characterize and qualify.

### Die-level voltage tuning for performance and power efficiency

Parametric variation affects how much voltage is required in one area compared to another to reach the same clock frequency.

It's standard practice in the industry to address this with *tuning*, which involves first understanding a die's relative response to voltage, then applying a specific voltage that allows the die to reach the target frequency.

Since conventional chips are manufactured as separate dies on a silicon wafer, they are cut and tuned individually. But in the WSE, since we keep all dies intact as a single chip, this represents a risk of being beholden to the worst-performing die (i.e., we need to apply the highest voltage that "passes" all of the dies).

We overcome this on our wafer with *die-level* tuning by providing each die its own regulator:

At a shared 0.70 V, A passes timing and B fails. At a shared 0.75 V, both pass, but A receives more voltage than it needs. That small voltage difference has a measurable power cost. Holding frequency and switching activity constant, dynamic power scales approximately with voltage squared:

(0.75 ÷ 0.70)² ≈ 1.15

Raising A from 0.70 V to 0.75 V therefore increases its dynamic power by roughly 15%, without increasing its clock frequency.

Local tuning works in both directions. We can raise B’s voltage to meet timing while lowering A’s voltage to save power. Relative to running both at 0.75 V, returning A to 0.70 V reduces its dynamic power by approximately 13%.

Assuming equal switching activity and capacitance at the same frequency, local tuning reduces their combined dynamic power by approximately 6.4% compared with running both at 0.75 V. This helps accommodate both ends of the manufactured population. Regions that need additional voltage can receive it without imposing the same power penalty everywhere else, and regions that meet timing at lower voltage retain their efficiency advantage.

### **Sensing and tracking all stages of hardware life**

Voltage tuning and the broader work of accommodating variation depend on precise measurements of our hardware. The WSE has 184 sensor blocks per reticle, distributed throughout the compute fabric. During manufacturing, ring oscillators measure local circuit speed, helping engineers map variation across the wafer and establish operating settings alongside functional tests.

Workload transitions create rapid changes in current demand that can cause supply voltage to droop or overshoot. Droop detectors flag voltage dips, delay-chain monitors track the effect on circuit speed, and an on-chip sampling array captures voltage waveforms for analysis. Together, they expose brief electrical disturbances that average readings would miss.

The WSE automatically controls the rate of change in activity. This lets it transition into quieter, cooler states while limiting voltage excursions. Engineers use the sensor readings to characterize demanding workloads and verify the hardware’s response.

### **Designed for years of service**

Operating conditions influence how quickly silicon ages. The same decisions that contribute to our yields help the hardware last out in the field. Voltage tuning keeps dies running at ideal conditions, preventing unnecessary stress. Our mass core design also addresses new defects that may emerge over the lifespan of the chip.

### In-field repair

The WSE’s spare cores and redundant connections serve two purposes. During manufacturing, they let us assemble the required compute fabric from silicon containing defects. After deployment, the remaining reserve can recover additional failures without physically replacing the wafer. Using the same redundant cores during defect routing, we can restore the original software-visible compute fabric without removing the wafer from the system.

Broad timing margins, local voltage control and fine-grained repair enable a larger percentage of the manufactured wafers to meet the product requirements. After deployment, those same controls and remaining redundant resources continue to protect useful compute capacity.

### Putting it all together

Chip design is an exercise in tradeoffs. Chip size, core count, and memory architecture are chosen for different outcomes. Simply enlarging an existing architecture leaves it exposed to more defects and variation. Making wafer scale economical requires designing around these realities from the very start.

These practices, in aggregate, are essential for wafer-scale computing. For single-die chip manufacturers, the same tradeoffs wouldn't make sense since there are other, simpler techniques that can be used to bring chips to market. At Cerebras, excellent attention to process and design serves as the foundation that makes our dies work across a broad range of conditions as one unit. The additional *cost* of the WSE is not in the manufacturing of the chip. It is in the time spent in the design room.
