# Fair use benefits everyone

Authors, journalists, creators, academics, accessibility advocates, legal experts, librarians, economists and business leaders are voicing their support for the longstanding principle of fair use and its application to AI training.


For decades, fair use has played a critical role in driving innovation that benefits everyone. It has helped deliver important medical and scientific breakthroughs and new technologies that have made real and positive differences for countless people, including authors and journalists. [The New York Times’ lawsuit](https://openai.com/new-york-times/), meanwhile, asks everyone to ignore a half century of legal history and progress, and toss aside the longstanding principle of fair use in their own narrow pursuit of an undeserved payday.

Experts and leaders across a wide range of fields have recognized that fair use is a win-win that benefits everyone, and have shared their perspectives with the Court in filing briefs in support of fair use.

Below you’ll find excerpts from some of those submissions, in their own words. The full filings are available on the public court docket, and you can read more about fair use and AI [here](https://openai.com/new-york-times/).

#### AI Progress

The Court should reject Plaintiffs’ attempt to extend copyright beyond its established limits. AI training uses copyrighted works for a fundamentally different purpose, does not provide the public with substitute access to those works, and does not cause the market harm cognizable under the Copyright Act. Instead, it enables a broad array of transformative technologies that are already delivering public benefits. Copyright exists to promote creativity and progress, not to suppress lawful innovation or protect authors from competition. Settled fair use principles therefore compel the conclusion that generative AI training is fair use.

#### American Council of the Blind, Association of Research Libraries, American Library Association, Consumer Technology Association & Business Software Alliance

This case concerns more than companies that develop large language models (LLMs). It affects medical researchers and patients, educators serving students with different learning needs, people with disabilities seeking greater independence, farmers applying agricultural knowledge, independent creators developing new forms of expression, and industry sectors from software to the law. These communities share an interest in the ability to study and learn from broad and diverse sources of knowledge, and in access to the tools that make that learning useful. Many depend on systems developed by others, including open source AI systems, rather than training models themselves. Fair use supports their ability to learn, discover, communicate, and create. Restrictions on fair use training would affect both the institutions building AI and the people using it.

#### Authors Alliance

The market dilution theory asks copyright law to protect rightsholders against an economic phenomenon that it has never restricted before. New works can reduce the sales, visibility, or value of older works without copying their protected expression. The Copyright Act deliberately leaves that competition outside the copyright owner’s control. Amicus therefore respectfully asks the Court to hold that “market dilution,” i.e., aggregate competitive pressure from noninfringing outputs, including those that occupy the same subject matter or genre, that share unprotected characteristics, or compete for the same audience, is not cognizable market harm under 17 U.S.C. § 107(4). Rejecting the theory would preserve the distinction the Second Circuit has always maintained: copyright protects authors against substitution for their protected expression, not against the success of new expression that merely competes with it.

#### Engine and Developers Alliance

Startups’ use of foundation models demonstrates that using copyrighted works to train an AI model is a fair use, not copyright infringement. The purpose of training a foundation model is not to reproduce the works it learns; it is to build a general-purpose tool that others can adapt to new ends. The ends startups have chosen bear no resemblance to the purposes of the works in any training dataset.

That reasoning supports a novel application here: considering the national-security value of capabilities that enable new analysis, software, and other productive work.

#### Joshua S. Gans

*Professor of Strategic Management*

Copyright supports not only the incentive to create content but also the incentive to build tools that learn from that content, and a rule that treated every displaced sale as actionable can weaken the second without reliably protecting the first.

#### Kevin Kelly

*Author*

AI is a transformative technology that accelerates the creation of novel works across countless disciplines in the arts and sciences. Mr. Kelly has experience in how AI advances the field of literature. In his view, each AI model can be understood as an entirely new medium that offers authors the capability to create new texts and ideas, and to reason. A ruling for Plaintiffs on fair use would therefore grant Plaintiffs far more than the limited monopoly to their original expression Congress intended to confer. It would hinder the development of technologies capable of disseminating previously unimaginable quantities of information. It would also impede new modes of thought and the creation of new original works in both the arts and sciences. Amicus files this brief in support of Defendants’ argument that the use of copyrighted works to train large language models is a fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. Based on his lifelong pursuits of writing, the protections afforded to copyright owners are important to Mr. Kelly. Equally vital is preserving room for permissible uses of copyrighted material and the growth of transformative technologies. Otherwise, copyright will stifle the very creativity, innovation, and spread of knowledge it exists to encourage. The fair-use doctrine is critical to ensuring that balance.

On the record in this case, the defendants’ use of plaintiffs’ works for AI training is highly transformative and strongly favors a finding of fair use because under realistic conditions the outputs of defendants’ models are in no relevant way similar to the invisible intermediate copies made during the training process.

#### Michael Wall and Sarah Rose Siskind

*Musician and composer / comedian and writer*

This brief offers a window into what that sentence means in practice through the work of two independent creators who rely on copyright protection *and *AI tools to sustain their livelihoods. *Amici *Sarah Rose Siskind and Michael Wall are artists who create and license original works. But they use AI to handle work surrounding the creative process and to grow their businesses in a market teeming with better-resourced competitors. For *Amici*, AI does not displace creativity or shortcut their creative processes. AI helps them create more, and to extend their work into new modes of expression. *Amici*’s experience underscores the United States’ view that AI tools “can and should help level the playing field between mainstream and independent publishers.” Siskind uses AI in every aspect of her business, from day-to-day administrative tasks to developing ideas and bringing in new clients. With AI, her noncreative work is faster and less expensive, and her creative work is better. As she tells it, “I don’t think I could run my business without AI at this point.” Similarly, AI helps Wall manage the operational demands of his music business, reduce costs, and devote more of his time to composing music. Like Siskind, he doubts that his business could survive in its current form without AI.

#### Public Knowledge

Fair use has protected tools that help people navigate the vast collections of information the digital world has brought us, including image search and full-text book search. See Perfect 10, Inc. v. Amazon.com, Inc., 508 F.3d 1146, 1164–68 (9th Cir. 2007); Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 216–17, 225 (2d Cir. 2015) (“Google Books”). It has also protected libraries’ provision of accessible digital books to readers with print disabilities. See Authors Guild, Inc. v. HathiTrust, 755 F.3d 87, 101–03 (2d Cir. 2014). And it has protected free online publication of technical standards incorporated into law, recognizing the “substantial public benefits of free and easy access to the law.” Am. Soc’y for Testing & Materials v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc., 82 F.4th 1262, 1271–72 (D.C. Cir. 2023). Fair use will be equally beneficial in ensuring that AI’s benefits accrue to the public at large, instead of being controlled by a privileged few.

#### Ron Suskind and Brian L. Frye

*Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist / Professor of Law*

OpenAI’s use of newspaper articles to train its generative AI models didn’t harm the reasonable commercial expectations of the News Plaintiffs in any way. They commissioned and published newspaper articles with the expectation of selling consumers access to those articles. They retain and continue to exercise the exclusive right to sell consumers access to their articles. Their ability to sell consumers access to their articles wasn’t affected by OpenAI’s use of those articles to train its AI models.

#### United States Government

In this litigation, the New York Times seeks to narrow fair-use doctrine to exclude the training of OpenAI’s large language models (LLMs). That result would be inconsistent with basic copyright law principles and severely hamper “the Progress of Science and useful Arts.” U.S. CONST. art. I, § 8, cl. 8. As discussed below, publications—including independent and start-up media, in addition to the New York Times itself—are leveraging LLMs for journalistic and other purposes. And beyond the subject matter of this litigation, LLMs are already helping researchers across fields achieve major breakthroughs. Constraining LLM development under a misunderstanding of fair use doctrine would thwart such creative and scientific progress while hindering American prosperity and economic mobility.
