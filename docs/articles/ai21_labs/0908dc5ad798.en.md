*This blog is authored by Assaf Gerner, Guy Rephaeli, Asaf Karnieli, Guy Freund, Shaked Meirom and Joanna Kramer, in collaboration with Alice’s Liran Cohen and Dadi Atar.*

Over just three weeks, our team of Forward-Deployed Researchers (FDRs) worked alongside Alice’s research team to post-train the recently released [NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Super VL](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/nemotron-3-5-super-vl/) to improve its resilience to attacks such as indirect prompt injection and malicious requests. The results demonstrate how specialized open models can surpass frontier-level quality at a fraction of the cost:

- **41-point** **improvement** on attack resilience as compared to the base model
- **10-point improvement** as compared to GPT-6.1 Sol
- **25x cheaper** than GPT-6.1 Sol (cost per 1M tokens)

**Figure 1: Our training recipe lifted Nemotron 3.5 Super VL from comparable in quality and cost to GPT-6.1 Luna to surpassing GPT-6.1 Sol in quality at a fraction of the cost. **

This post details Alice’s use case and presents the custom training recipe we developed to achieve frontier-level results on Alice’s use case.

## Improving agent resilience to attacks with Alice

[Alice](https://alice.io/), an AI safety and security company, builds RL Environments for post training that are informed by real-world intelligence on threat actor behavior (the “Rabbit Hole”) and input from 150+ staff security researchers and expert network members. They provided AI21 with a set of RL gyms to train agents to defend against security threats across their enterprise environments, including platforms, systems, tools, data, and workflows, such as Slack and GitHub. The goal is to produce agents that score highly on both resistance to attacks and task completion – so that one aspect does not come at the expense of the other.

*Figure 2: An example Slack task from Alice’s gym. Company and domain names are fictional.*

One kind of these attacks is indirect prompt injection – malicious instructions planted in information an agent reads with tools. For example, an agent might be prompted to send a password reset reminder to employees on Slack – but the reset link is corrupted, risking exposing employee login credentials. An agent trained in Alice’s RL gyms will learn to detect that breach and prevent it, while still accomplishing its original task: notifying employees to reset their passwords (Figure 2).

As more enterprises integrate agents into their core workspaces – which entails high levels of access to sensitive information, as well as widespread access to employees – ensuring that these agents are equipped to spot and stop attacks becomes paramount. Our work post-training Nemotron 3.5 Super VL on Alice’s RL gyms proves that it’s possible for an agent to maintain high attack resilience alongside task completion.

## The challenge

To successfully post-train Nemotron 3.5 Super to excel on this use case, our FDRs had to address two core challenges.

The first challenge was how to improve attack resilience without hurting task completion on regular benign tasks. To take the example from above, if the Slack agent refuses to stop working after it detects the malicious reset link, it has successfully defended the company – yet is ultimately not a very useful agent.

The second challenge was around data. Given that the training dataset included several hundred examples, the team needed to find a way to train without overfitting the model to the training data.

They designed the post-training process to resolve these challenges.

## Methodology

### Training the agent inside Alice’s RL gyms

Addressing both challenges meant training Nemotron 3.5 Super the way it will actually be used: as an agent acting inside a live environment, not as a model answering isolated prompts. Alice’s gyms are packaged as Harbor tasks [1], and each task comes with its own container image holding a simulated workspace, such as Slack or GitHub.

For every rollout, the AI21 Training Platform launches a fresh, isolated sandbox from that image. The model then works through the task inside the sandbox: it reads messages, calls tools and takes actions. When the episode ends, the gym scores the outcome. Giving each rollout its own sandbox keeps training secure, because anything the agent does in response to an attack stays inside the sandbox. It also lets us run hundreds of rollouts in parallel. We use this same setup throughout: to generate the trajectories for SFT, to produce the live rollouts for GRPO, and to evaluate every checkpoint.

### Warm-starting with near-policy SFT

Online reinforcement learning has become the standard approach for post-training language models. Because it is on-policy, the model learns from rollouts it generates during training rather than from a dataset collected in advance. Training on its own outputs improves quality and reduces the risk of catastrophic forgetting. The most widely used online RL method, GRPO, samples a group of rollouts for each prompt and compares them, so the model learns what separates better outputs from worse ones. This means GRPO learns only when the rollouts in a group receive different rewards. If every rollout in a group gets the same score, every advantage is zero, the group contributes no gradient, and nothing is learned.

However, in this case, when we ran the initial evaluation of Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, we found the model produced rollouts with very low diversity scores. Therefore, before we could move to RL, they first ran a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage to raise the fraction of tasks with non-zero reward variance within a group [2]. After SFT, the share of tasks from the training set with non-zero reward standard deviation rose from 35% to 66%.

We generated successful rollouts on the gyms from several open models, yet training on them as-is presented two problems. First, some of these models reason in very long chains that sometimes fall into repetitive loops (Figure 4), a habit that we didn’t want Nemotron 3.5 to pick up. Second, the SFT data should teach safe behavior, not another model’s writing style. More broadly, data close to the policy’s own distribution is easier to learn and causes less forgetting [3], so even at the SFT stage we strived to keep training data as on-policy as possible (Figure 3). To do this, they had Nemotron 3.5 Super rewrite the reasoning in each trajectory in its own words, which shortened long chains while leaving every action unchanged [3,4].

Figure 3 shows the effect. As-is, the open-model rollouts were nearly twice as surprising to the base model as its own rollouts (×1.99 perplexity). A policy fine-tuned on them drifted to ×1.40, while a policy fine-tuned on the rephrased rollouts stayed closer, at ×1.23. Rephrasing cut the added perplexity from 40% to 23%.

Lower perplexity means the rollouts are closer to the base model’s own distribution. Values are shown relative to the base model’s perplexity on its own rollouts (reference). The fix was to have Nemotron 3.5 Super rewrite the reasoning in each trajectory in its own words. Doing so shortened long chains while leaving every action unchanged [3,4].

**Figure 4: Rephrasing the SFT data halves the checkpoint’s reasoning length and nearly eliminates the most common repetitive fillers (e.g., “Hmm. OK”).**

The SFT checkpoint trained on the rewritten data matched the quality of the one trained on the original rollouts, while generating far fewer tokens and not regressing in other tasks.

### Reward shaping in online RL

Once we had established a good SFT trained Nemotron-3.5-Super VL checkpoint, with improved results and more diverse rollouts, we were able to continue to the second phase: online RL training using GRPO.

Vanilla GRPO computes each rollout’s advantage in two steps: it subtracts the group’s mean reward, then divides by the group’s standard deviation. We quickly found that the second step was a poor fit for Alice’s RL gyms, which use dense, continuous rewards. Dividing by the group’s spread throws away the size of the reward gap. For example, a group with rewards [0.49, 0.51] and a group with rewards [0, 1] both end up with advantages [−1, +1]. The ranking survives, but the magnitude is lost. With dense rewards, this means tiny and possibly noisy differences, such as a slight shift in an LLM judge’s score, get amplified into full-strength gradients [6,9]. We therefore dropped the standard-deviation normalization and kept only the group-mean baseline (Figure 5).

Another reward modification was needed to keep response length under control. Response length often grows during RL [5,6]. However, in this use case it grew sharply, and some rollouts looped until they used up the context window. To counter this, we ablated two additional reward terms: (1) a group-relative length penalty, which penalizes the longest rollouts within each group [7,8], and (2) an n-gram repetition penalty, which penalizes rollouts whose reasoning repeats the same n-gram many times [5]. Indeed, the combination of these three modifications yielded the best results – reaching the highest quality, while generating the shortest response length. (Figure 5).

## Results

*Figure 6: In-distribution scores on the validation set and its GitHub and Slack subsets at each training stage, from the base model through SFT to GRPO.*

The post-trained model was evaluated on two different sets. Using two lets us check whether its gains reflect real safety behavior rather than overfitting to the training environments. The first is a validation set, which uses the same GitHub and Slack environments as training and measures in-distribution performance. The second is CHASM, Alice’s Enterprise Indirect Prompt Injection (IPI) benchmark, designed as an out-of-distribution gym comprising 117 attack tasks across enterprise workflows spanning domains such as finance, HR, legal, and customer support, as well as industries from healthcare to telecom.

CHASM shares no tasks or apps with the RL gyms, so it tests whether that behavior generalizes. Its attacks, mostly indirect prompt injection and poisoned records, try to push the agent into exfiltrating data, breaching confidentiality or diverting payments.

On the validation set, each stage builds on the last (Figure 6). SFT nearly doubles the score, from 35.19 to 65.28, and GRPO lifts it to 76.2. Most of the GRPO gain is on GitHub (66.48 → 85.17), while Slack improves only slightly (64.08 → 67.22).

On CHASM, the base model scores just 7.54. SFT raises it to 34.58, mainly by teaching the model to avoid the harmful action. GRPO holds that gain but doesn’t add to it. The safe behavior learned in SFT carries over to unseen environments and attack types, while the gains from GRPO stay within the training distribution.

## Reaching frontier-level attack resilience with a custom post-training recipe

Together, AI21 and Alice improved Nemotron 3.5 Super VL’s attack resilience by 41 points above the base model and 10 points above GPT-6.1 Sol without harming overall task performance – and while costing 25x less than the frontier. To achieve these results, AI21’s FDR team sequentially applied on-policy SFT, followed by a modified GRPO (online RL) technique.

Their work demonstrates that companies can train open models that integrate their core safety and security policies as core behavioral principles, ensuring they complete their assigned tasks while spotting and resisting threats, such as prompt injection attacks.
