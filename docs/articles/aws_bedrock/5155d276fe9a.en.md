# Getting AI out of the demo and into businesses globally

*AWS Forward Deployed Engineering is now available in 15 countries including U.S., Mexico, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Singapore, India, South Korea, Japan, UK, Germany, Netherlands, France, Sweden, and Saudi Arabia for commercial and public sector customers; and powered by dozens of built in production-ready accelerators.*

Every enterprise I talk to has built something with AI that works. A prototype that reads a claim. An agent that resolves a card dispute. A model that answers a customer question in seconds.

The prototype works. The demo impresses stakeholders. Then the organization tries to put it into production, and the real problems begin. Production data is messier than the test set. Auditors want to know why the agent made each decision. Volume grows from a pilot group to the whole company. The rules that matter most turn out to live in people’s heads.

None of these are model problems. They’re business problems, and they decide whether AI ever pays off. The stretch from demo to production is the last mile, where most AI stalls. It’s also where our teams spend their time.

We launched AWS Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) in June to close that gap. Since then, we’ve worked through these problems with commercial and public sector customers in financial services, healthcare, sports, media, retail, and more. Here’s what we’ve learned, and how it shaped what we built.

## The problem has three layers

Model providers, cloud providers, and consulting firms are all embedding engineers with customers. At AWS FDE, we measure success by what remains after our engineers leave: a foundation your team owns, workflows that keep learning, and governance you can trust.

Across customers, the hardest problems show up in three layers: the foundation, the workflows, and governance. Each one is harder than it looks, and each one builds on the one before it. That holds whether you’re starting fresh or building on existing systems. On a greenfield build, you can move fast, but there’s no foundation to stand on yet. On a brownfield system, agents must work within decades of data, rules, and integrations.

## Layer one: A foundation that compounds

Before an agent can do useful work at scale, it needs a foundation: identity, security, context, governance, and operational controls. That foundation must be production-ready from day one and shared by every use case that follows. Context is often the biggest gap. Most customers have data spread across systems and no common ontology, the shared model of how the business works that agents need to reason over.

Without a shared foundation, the second pilot costs nearly as much as the first. Integrations get rebuilt, and lessons fail to carry forward. Most organizations reach a point where they stop and build the foundation first, so each new use case starts from what the last one built instead of from scratch. We call this the AI reset.

Samsung Life Insurance had four affiliates, each building its own AI point solutions from scratch. In 45 days, the FDE team built a governed agentic platform with a single control plane and compliance built in. New agents now deploy through registration and policy assignment instead of a rebuild.

## Layer two: Automating workflows

Next is the work itself: High-volume, rule-heavy processes such as claims processing, know your customer (KYC) reviews, card disputes, loan origination, invoice reconciliation, permitting, and customer service. This is where AI can deliver immediate and measurable outcomes. The payoff is fast, but the knowledge that drives these processes rarely lives in a system. It lives with the people who do the work.

The hard part isn’t automation itself. It is uncovering the business rules behind each decision. When an invoice and a purchase order disagree, which record wins? When can an exception clear on its own, and when does it need a person? Experienced employees answer these questions from judgment, and that judgment is rarely written down.

Sometimes the knowledge is buried in the systems themselves.

Rio Tinto Iron Ore runs one of the world’s most complex mine-to-ship supply chains on a 30-year-old Oracle system. That system has more than 5,000 database tables and 140 applications. Mapping it had been a multi-month exercise. The FDE team built agents that mapped it in weeks, surfacing the dependencies that Rio Tinto’s teams could validate and build against.

Mapping what exists is only the start. The biggest opportunities emerge when organizations stop asking how AI can make existing work more efficient and start asking how it can change how work gets done.

## Layer three: Governance that holds up as everything changes

Governance isn’t a phase of the project. It’s part of the architecture. As agents multiply, so do security risks, costs, and decisions you must explain. Every agent needs its own identity, permissions, and audit trail, separate from human identity. And because the models underneath are probabilistic, agents need monitoring that keeps their results predictable and auditable long after launch.

The system also must keep working as everything around it changes. Models drift, business rules shift, data formats change, and regulations tighten. New security issues appear, and code falls out of date. As newer models arrive, the instructions and tooling around them will change too. You need evaluations and guardrails that keep agents predictable where they must be and creative where they can be.

When governance comes at the end, it becomes the gate that stops production. When it’s built in from day one, it’s what lets your business move fast.

Marsh, a leading global professional services firm, needed agents that would hold up under its own audit standards. The FDE team built those controls in from the start, and the first use case reached production about two months after work began. Efficiency gains in the targeted areas have been as much as 70 percent, with some process steps moving from hours to seconds. Marsh has since expanded the work and keeps extending the system itself.

We refine it with your business stakeholders, then build in your environment from day one, on your data and through your identity and security controls. Your context is what makes scaling possible. Say your first use case is a sales automation workflow. Because its context lives in your AWS account, the next use case, supply chain optimization, builds on it instead of starting over. After the first or second project, we want you running AI software factories of your own. One financial services customer did exactly that with AI-DLC, with agents and engineers building together. Throughput rose 53%, and code reviews got 56% faster.

You also keep your choices open. Models are improving fast.
